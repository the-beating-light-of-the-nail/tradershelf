If the stop is hit, the loss lands on — regardless of how big the position is. That is the whole point.

How the math works

Position sizing starts from the loss, not the position. First fix the amount you'd accept losing on this trade — say 1% of a $10,000 account, or $100. Then divide that amount by the distance between your entry and your stop. If you enter at 50 with a stop at 48, each unit risks 2, so you can hold 50 units. A stop-out then costs exactly 50 × 2 = $100.

The direction is inferred from your prices: an entry above the stop means you're long (a stop protects the downside); an entry below it means you're short (a stop protects the upside). The formula is identical either way, because only the absolute distance matters.

  • Works for any market — shares, crypto, futures, forex. Prices just need to share one currency.
  • Fractional sizes are fine — 0.034 BTC is a valid answer. Round down if your broker can't do fractions.
  • Slippage isn't included — real fills can overshoot the stop. Many traders size slightly under the formula for that reason.

Common questions

What is position sizing in trading?

It's deciding how large a trade should be before entry. Instead of guessing a number of shares or contracts, you start from the amount you're willing to lose if the stop is hit, then work backwards to a size. This calculator does that working-backwards for you.

What percentage of my account should I risk per trade?

Many traders keep single-trade risk between 0.5% and 2%. There's no universal number — the purpose of a fixed percentage is that a losing streak stays survivable. Ten straight 1% losses draw the account down about 9.6%, not 10%, because each loss is a percentage of a smaller account.

Does this work for crypto and forex?

Yes. The math is unit-agnostic: enter prices in your market's quote currency and your account size in the same currency. The result is a size in base units — BTC, shares, contracts, or lots alike.
