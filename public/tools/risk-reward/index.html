How to read it

The ratio compares the distance from entry to target against the distance from entry to stop. A 1:2 setup risks one unit to make two. Direction is inferred from your prices: target above entry with stop below means long; the reverse means short. The math is identical either way.

The break-even win rate is the quiet hero of the number. It's the share of trades you must win to end up at exactly zero, ignoring fees: 1 ÷ (1 + R). At 1:2 you only need to be right a third of the time; at 1:1 you need half; at 2:1 — risking twice what you target — you need to be right two times out of three, which is a very different game.

  • Lower R:R isn't automatically worse — tight-target strategies with high hit rates can be profitable. The break-even number tells you which game you're playing.
  • Fees aren't included — commissions and spread raise the effective bar. Give yourself a cushion.
  • Targets are guesses, stops are orders — the stop is the one price here you can enforce mechanically.

Common questions

What is a good risk:reward ratio?

There's no magic number, but many traders look for at least 1:2. Lower ratios can still be profitable — they just demand a higher win rate, which the break-even figure above makes explicit. Judge the ratio and your realistic hit rate together, not separately.

What is the break-even win rate?

The win rate at which a strategy makes exactly zero, ignoring fees. It equals 1 ÷ (1 + R). At 1:2 you break even winning 33.3% of trades; at 1:1 you need 50%; at 1:3 only 25%.

What does 1R mean?

1R is the amount you risk on a trade — one unit of risk. A +2R winner on a $100 risk makes $200; a −1R loser loses the $100. Quoting results in R keeps strategy quality separate from position size and account size.
